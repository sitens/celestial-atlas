About · sources · self-test

Every pixel and number is a pure function of one master timestamp. Positions come from Astronomy Engine (Don Cross, MIT) — VSOP87-class planets, a Moon theory, Jupiter's Galilean moons — accurate to about an arcminute for 1700–2200 CE. Other moons use JPL SSD mean elements (epoch J2000; “mean-element accuracy”: good for visuals, not for precise phenomena). Planet orientation uses IAU WGCCRE rotation models. Eclipse shadows are computed analytically per pixel from the true Sun/occluder geometry (exact disc-overlap), so umbra and penumbra land where the ephemeris says. Eclipse times/contacts come from Astronomy Engine's search routines, not from animation.

Limits. 1700–2200 CE: no warning. 1000–1700 and 2200–3000: allowed with a “reduced accuracy” banner. Outside 1000–3000 the picker is disabled. Past dates show the proleptic Gregorian calendar; local time uses today's IANA rules so pre-1970 civil times may differ from history. Star positions are J2000 positions moved by proper motion and precessed to the date. Earth city-lights and cloud textures are present-day. Planet surfaces, ring shadows and moon shadows on Jupiter use real geometry, but body sizes in “Explorable” scale are exaggerated.

Data & credits. Geocoding & weather: Open-Meteo (CC-BY 4.0; forecast, ERA5 historical archive). Planet & Milky Way textures: Solar System Scope (CC BY 4.0, NASA-derived). Earth, Moon, clouds, city lights: three.js example textures (NASA Visible Earth / Blue Marble derived). Hubble galaxies: NASA/ESA (via Wikimedia Commons): NGC 4414, M51, NGC 1300, M104, Antennae. Gaia DR3: ESA/Gaia/DPAC. Star & constellation data: Yale Bright Star Catalogue via d3-celestial (BSD). Satellite elements: NASA/JPL SSD. Rendering: three.js (MIT).

Helical motion (Solar System view, “Helix” chip). The Sun carries the whole system at ≈ 246 km/s (≈ 52 AU every year) around the Galactic Centre, toward Galactic l ≈ 87°, b ≈ 2° (Cygnus). In the Galaxy's frame each planet therefore traces a corkscrew (the Moon a smaller one around Earth's). Trails are real positions from the ephemeris, displaced along the Sun's velocity; the along-track stretch is exaggerated (slider) because the true pitch is ≈ 52× the orbit radius — the panel states the factor. In “True scale” the helix is drawn at its real length. In the default scale the Moon's orbit is drawn compact from afar (so it can't cross the orbits of Venus or Mars) and opens to full size as you approach Earth.

Beyond the Milky Way. The NASA/JPL-Caltech/R. Hurt (SSC-Caltech) artist's concept of the Milky Way (public domain, via Wikimedia Commons) is shown beneath the model stars (toggle “NASA art”); it is an illustration, not a photograph — nobody has seen our Galaxy from outside. Spiral arms rotate rigidly at an adjustable pattern speed Ωp (20–32 km/s/kpc; the literature spans this range), and the strip shows when the Sun would be inside an arm (half-width 0.5 kpc, model-dependent). Any link between arm passages and mass extinctions or ice ages is a hypothesis, not a settled result. Neighbour and Local Group positions are catalogue values (McConnachie 2012; NED); the Laniakea view is schematic: cluster positions are catalogue values, the filaments and streamlines are illustrative of the flow toward the Great Attractor (Tully et al. 2014).

Galaxy view. A schematic Milky Way: 110,000 procedural stars on four logarithmic spiral arms (13° pitch, crossing the Sun line near Reid+2019 positions), a 27° bar and a bulge. The Sun's orbit is integrated in a flat-rotation-curve potential (R₀ 8.18 kpc, vc 233 km/s, solar motion U,V,W = 11.1, 12.24, 7.25 km/s): one galactic year ≈ 223 Myr, radial swing 8.1–9.2 kpc, vertical oscillation ±85 pc every ≈ 70 Myr. The Sun's vertical wobble is multiplied (slider) so it is visible; the disc itself is drawn at its true thickness (a ~0.3 kpc scale height — about 1:50 against its 16 kpc radius), with a flared outer disc, a thick disc, a boxy/peanut bulge, an outer warp, a stellar halo and dust lanes, so edge-on it is the thin, bulging, warped disc astronomers see in other galaxies. “Helix through space” adds the Galaxy's ≈ 560 km/s motion relative to the CMB (Sun’s 370 km/s dipole minus its orbital velocity; toward l ≈ 266°, b ≈ 29°) — the path then winds with a pitch of ≈ 125 kpc per galactic year (drawn at 20% by default so the coils show; the Drift slider goes to the real 100%).

Real stars and real galaxy shapes. Around the Sun, “Stars” shows 50,146 real stars from ESA Gaia DR3 (a 1-in-12 random sample of the 600,000 stars brighter than G = 10.5 within 1 kpc; CC BY-SA 3.0 IGO) at their measured 3-D positions, with the nearest named stars from the Hipparcos-derived HYG database. The neighbouring galaxies are 3-D point clouds, tilted to their catalogue inclinations and position angles (Andromeda 77.5° / 37.7°, Triangulum 56°, the Large Magellanic Cloud 35°, the Small Cloud stretched along our line of sight, dwarf spheroidals as ellipsoids); stars within them are procedural — only the shapes and orientations are data-driven. “Planets” zooms through to the Solar System, where the helix lives.

Milky Way – Andromeda collision. The Collision scale runs a restricted N-body simulation in your browser: 13,000 stars (Milky Way and Andromeda discs, bulges and haloes) move as test particles in two softened, truncated isothermal dark-matter haloes whose centres follow a two-body orbit with Chandrasekhar dynamical friction. Andromeda starts at its observed position and approach speed (≈ 110 km/s radial, 28 km/s tangential). The orbit is tuned to published timelines (first pericentre ≈ 3.6 Gyr from now at ≈ 30 kpc, second ≈ 5.6 Gyr, merged ≈ 6.4 Gyr; Cox & Loeb 2008, van der Marel et al. 2012, Sohn et al. 2012). It is illustrative: gas, star formation, M33 and the true halo masses are not modelled. The yellow ring is a test star started on the Sun’s orbit.

The Sun’s life. “Sun’s life” (Solar System view) shows luminosity, radius, mass and temperature from birth (−4.57 Gyr) to the white dwarf stage, using the published tracks of Sackmann, Boothroyd & Kraemer (1993) and Schröder & Smith (2008: red-giant tip at +7.59 Gyr, 256 R☉, 2,730 L☉, 0.67 M☉; final white dwarf 0.54 M☉). The habitable zone uses the simple flux limits 1.107 and 0.356 S⊕; orbits widen as the Sun loses mass. Distances are square-root scaled and planets enlarged. Earth’s fate (swallowed through tidal drag) is debated.

Moving around. Drag to orbit, scroll or pinch to zoom. To pan: ✥ Move (one-finger/left drag), right-drag, Shift-drag, two-finger drag, or W A S D; ⌖ Recenter (C) flies back. The Gaia “1-in-4” sample inside 100 pc (92,000 stars) fades in when you zoom close to the Sun; the Gould Belt is a schematic ellipse after Pöppel (1997).

Guide, narration and tips. The Guide describes what is on screen right now (from the live ephemeris), suggests what to try, and can read aloud (your browser's speech synthesis; off by default). Hover or long-press any button for a tooltip that shows its live effect.
